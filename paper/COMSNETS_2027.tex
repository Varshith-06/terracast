\section{Proposed Approach}
\label{sec:approach}

The proposed framework models terrain--vehicle interaction as a comparison between the rating
cone index (RCI) of the soil and the vehicle cone index (VCI) of each vehicle, both in psi.
Since soil strength changes from day to day, the framework forecasts RCI from a local weather
log, turns it into per-vehicle trafficability maps, and plans routes for the day of travel
rather than the day of planning (shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}).

\subsection{Terrain--Vehicle Interaction Model}

\subsubsection{Soil Strength}
With the surface (0--7\,cm) and root-zone (7--28\,cm) soil moisture scaled to
$s^{(1)}_t, s^{(2)}_t \in [0,1]$ between 20 and 49\,vol\% (just outside the 20.7--48.9\,vol\%
range of the 11-year record), and $R^{3d}_t$ the rainfall of the last three days, the soil
wetness index (SWI) and soil strength on day $t$ are:
\begin{gather}
  \mathrm{SWI}_t = 0.35\,s^{(1)}_t + 0.65\,s^{(2)}_t , \\
  \mathrm{RCI}_t = 300\,e^{-k\,\mathrm{SWI}_t}
  \left(1 - 0.15\min\!\left(R^{3d}_t / 50\,\mathrm{mm},\ 1\right)\right),
  \label{eq:rci}
\end{gather}
where $k = \ln(300/15)$. As in the strength--moisture model used in~\cite{liu2025},
$\ln\mathrm{RCI}$ falls linearly with a moisture term, here the SWI. The constants are ours. The
air-dry strength of 300 lies in the 100--500\,psi range of excess strength beyond which NRMM
treats fine-grained soil as a hard surface~\cite{haley1979}, and the saturated strength of 15
follows field readings at immobilisation~\cite{rosca2018}. The deeper layer weighs more because
it covers both NRMM critical layers, 0--15\,cm for one pass and 15--30\,cm for 50
passes~\cite{haley1979}; weights of 0.25/0.75 or 0.5/0.5 change the daily class of at most 2\%
of vehicle-days. The rain factor covers free surface water, which daily soil moisture misses:
50\,mm is the top of the 25--50\,mm rainfall class of~\cite{liu2025}, and the 15\% cap is below
their 20--50\% rainfall reductions because soil moisture already carries most of the rain's
effect.

\subsubsection{Vehicle Cone Index}
Each vehicle is characterised by its Mobility Index (MI)~\cite{rula1971,haley1979}. For a
tracked vehicle:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MI} = \left(\frac{\mathrm{CPF}\cdot\mathrm{WF}}{\mathrm{TF}\cdot\mathrm{GF}}
  + \mathrm{BF} - \mathrm{CF}\right)\mathrm{EF}\cdot\mathrm{TrF},
\end{equation}
where CPF, WF, TF, GF, BF, CF, EF and TrF are the contact pressure, weight, track, grouser,
bogie, clearance, engine and transmission factors. MI gives the soil strength needed for one
pass and for 50 passes in the same track~\cite{ciobotaru2009}:
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{VCI}_1    &= 7.0 + 0.2\,\mathrm{MI} - 39.2/(\mathrm{MI} + 5.6), \\
  \mathrm{VCI}_{50} &= 19.27 + 0.43\,\mathrm{MI} - 125.79/(\mathrm{MI} + 7.08).
\end{align}
Wheeled vehicles use the wheeled forms, with tyre and wheel-load factors~\cite{rosca2017,rosca2018},
taking 15\% tyre deflection (deflection factor 1) and automatic transmissions. Half-tracks have no
published form: the track unit is rated as tracked and the front axle as wheeled with 25\% of the
weight, and the worse of the two governs.
As the vehicle data give only mass, engine power and top speed, the running gear is assumed:
track width, length, road wheels and clearance are interpolated by mass through the published
dimensions of six reference vehicles from the M22 to the Tiger~II, and tyre size follows the
load per axle through standard sizes from the HMMWV to heavy 8$\times$8 classes. These
assumptions are tested by the published cases: our implementation reproduces the published M1 example (MI 98.6, $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ 26.3,
$\mathrm{VCI}_{50}$ 60.5)~\cite{ciobotaru2009} and the 24 published values of six 8$\times$8
vehicles~\cite{rosca2017} to within 1.7\,psi. Vehicles are grouped into five bands of
$\mathrm{VCI}_1$, with edges set by the vehicle mix (A: light tracked, B--D: medium to heavy
tracked, E: wheeled), each planned with a representative vehicle: the median $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ and
$\mathrm{VCI}_{50}$ of the band and, for speed, the median power-to-weight ratio, contact
pressure and top speed of its majority running gear.

\subsubsection{Passes}
A vehicle is NO-GO when $\mathrm{RCI} < \mathrm{VCI}_1$, SLOW-GO when
$\mathrm{VCI}_1 \le \mathrm{RCI} < \mathrm{VCI}_{50}$, and GO otherwise. The three labels are
those of military terrain analysis~\cite{fm533}; NO-GO below $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ follows from its
definition~\cite{rula1971}, and placing GO at $\mathrm{VCI}_{50}$, where fifty vehicles can use one
track, is our mapping.
SLOW-GO is quantified by the number of vehicles one track can carry:
\begin{equation}
  n(\mathrm{RCI}) = 50^{(\mathrm{RCI} - \mathrm{VCI}_1)/(\mathrm{VCI}_{50} - \mathrm{VCI}_1)}.
  \label{eq:passes}
\end{equation}
Equation (\ref{eq:passes}) is ours: the sources give only the 1-pass and 50-pass
requirements~\cite{rula1971}, so the required strength is taken to rise with $\ln n$ between
them, giving 1 pass at $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ and 50 at $\mathrm{VCI}_{50}$.

\subsubsection{Speed}
The speed follows the fine-grained soil relations of the NATO Reference Mobility Model
(NRMM)~\cite{haley1979}, in which the motion resistance $R/W$, traction $T/W$ and slip $s$ of
the running gear are functions of the excess strength $\mathrm{RCI} - \mathrm{VCI}_1$. On a
slope $\theta$, the vehicle is stuck if $(T/W)\cos\theta < \sin\theta + (R/W)\cos\theta$;
otherwise its speed is where the rim pull meets the resistance:
\begin{equation}
  v = \min\!\left(\frac{\eta P\,(1 - s)}{W\left(\sin\theta + (R/W)\cos\theta\right)},\ v_{\max}\right),
  \label{eq:speed}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the engine power, $W$ the weight and $v_{\max}$ the top speed from the vehicle
table, and $\eta = 0.8$ is an assumed driveline efficiency, as the table has no powertrain data;
speed scales linearly with $\eta$, which does not affect NO-GO calls or passes. The constant-power rim pull $\eta P / v$ is ours, as NRMM builds
it gear by gear from engine data that the vehicle table lacks. On paved roads, the NRMM
hard-surface resistance replaces the soil terms; half-tracks use the tracked relations. The
NO-GO line is kept at $\mathrm{VCI}_1$, 0.7--2.1\,psi above NRMM's own level-ground
immobilisation point, which errs on the safe side. Two checks support the model: at
$\mathrm{VCI}_1$ the relations give 20\% slip, the slip at which NRMM defines $\mathrm{VCI}_1$,
and for the M1 they give 12\,km/h at $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ and 45\,km/h 20\,psi above it, comparable
to the published NRMM run for that vehicle, about 20\,km/h just before NO-GO and 56\,km/h at
most~\cite{ciobotaru2009}. As
NRMM's vegetation, ride and obstacle limits are not modelled, the speeds are upper bounds.

\subsection{GRU--Ridge Forecaster}
The forecaster uses only the local weather log, without numerical weather prediction. Its
inputs are 27 daily channels: RCI, SWI and the two soil-moisture layers, rainfall, Hargreaves
reference evapotranspiration~\cite{hargreaves1985} and the water balance, temperature, humidity,
wind, pressure and cloud cover, antecedent precipitation indices with decay 0.85 and 0.95 per
day~\cite{kohler1951}, days since rain, seasonal
harmonics and a monsoon flag. The calendar terms of the target days, known in advance, are
also given. As RCI has a lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.99, the forecaster predicts the change in
RCI for all lead times $h = 1, \dots, 7$ days at once:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathrm{RCI}}_{t+h} = \mathrm{RCI}_t
  + \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\bar g_h(\mathbf{X}_{t-59:t}) + r_h(\boldsymbol{\phi}_t)\right],
  \label{eq:forecast}
\end{equation}
where $\bar g_h$ is the mean of three gated recurrent unit (GRU) networks~\cite{cho2014}
(2 layers, 128 hidden units, the size used for all recurrent models in Table~\ref{tab:arch})
over a 60-day input window $\mathbf{X}$, over which the slowest antecedent-rain index (decay
0.95 per day) falls to 5\% of its weight, and $r_h$ is a ridge
regression~\cite{hoerl1970} on 128 causal features $\boldsymbol{\phi}_t$ (lags, rolling
statistics and seasonal terms), with its penalty chosen per lead time on the validation years.
Equation (\ref{eq:forecast}) is ours: predicting the change makes persistence the zero-change
forecast, and averaging a nonlinear sequence model with a linear feature model lets their
errors partly cancel. A vehicle of band $b$ is called NO-GO when:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathrm{RCI}}_{t+h} - m_{b,h} < \mathrm{VCI}_1 ,
  \label{eq:decision}
\end{equation}
where $m_{b,h}$ is the smallest safety margin, searched from 0 to 40\,psi in 0.25\,psi steps,
that catches 90\% of NO-GO days. This rule is ours: subtracting a margin biases the call
towards safety, and tuning it per band and lead time fixes the miss rate for each vehicle class.
The 90\% target is an operating point; Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff} shows the results at other
margins.
The GRUs are trained with AdamW optimizer, Batch Size: 128, Epochs: 300 with early stopping
(patience 30), Learning Rate: 0.001 with a one-cycle schedule, Weight Decay: 0.0001 and mean
squared error loss on targets standardised per lead time, each with a different random seed.
The forecaster has 531,228 parameters.

\subsection{Trafficability Mapping}
The terrain is a 90\,m grid built from 3$\times$3 blocks of a 30\,m Google Earth Engine export,
which keeps the routing graph at 76,218 cells:
the mean elevation and slope, the most common land-cover class, and the peak NDVI over three
seasons as canopy density; cells that are mostly water are impassable. The topographic wetness
index of each cell is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{TWI}_c = \ln\left(a_c / \tan\beta_c\right),
  \label{eq:twi}
\end{equation}
where $a_c$ is the upslope area from D8 flow accumulation~\cite{ocallaghan1984} and $\beta_c$
the local slope~\cite{beven1979}. It shifts the SWI of each cell by $\delta_c = 0.1\,\mathrm{clip}(z_c,
-2, 2)$, where $z_c$ is the z-score of its TWI, so that
$\mathrm{RCI}_c = \widehat{\mathrm{RCI}}\,e^{-k\delta_c}$ from~(\ref{eq:rci}). The scale is
ours: 0.1 SWI per standard deviation is 2.9\,vol\%, capped at 5.8\,vol\%, within the 3.6--7.1\,vol\%
spread of soil moisture measured from 2.5\,m to 50\,km~\cite{famiglietti2008}. The soil
membership of a cell is:
\begin{equation}
  \mu^{\mathrm{soil}}_c =
  \begin{cases}
    0, & \mathrm{RCI}_c < \mathrm{VCI}_1, \\
    0.25 + 0.75\min(\rho_c, 1), & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:soil}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_c = (\mathrm{RCI}_c - \mathrm{VCI}_1)/(\mathrm{VCI}_{50} - \mathrm{VCI}_1)$.
Equation (\ref{eq:soil}) is ours and follows the speed model: the NRMM speed~(\ref{eq:speed}) at
$\mathrm{VCI}_1$ is 17--25\% of that at $\mathrm{VCI}_{50}$ for the band vehicles, hence the
floor of 0.25, which keeps SLOW-GO ground slow rather than forbidden, and the linear rise is
within 0.1 of the NRMM curve at the midpoint. Slope and land-cover memberships follow the tables
of Liu et al.~\cite{liu2025} for tracked and wheeled vehicles. The slope steps are smoothed into
a monotone curve through the middle of each class, so that a small change in slope cannot flip
a decision. As 79\% of the plateau is tree cover, which~\cite{liu2025} scores as impassable,
forest is split by peak NDVI into their forest (dense, $\geq$0.75; 0 for all vehicles), bush-wood
(open, 0.60--0.75; 0 wheeled, 0.2 tracked) and grassland rows (sparse, $<$0.60; 0.4 wheeled,
0.6 tracked), where 0.75 is about the upper tercile of peak NDVI in forest cells; water is set
to 0. Before soil is considered, 26\% of the plateau is passable for wheeled and 72\% for tracked
vehicles. Roads come from
OpenStreetMap~\cite{osm}: classes from motorway to track are kept, and slope and land cover are
1 on them. Graded and asphalt roads are paved and ignore soil ($\mu^{\mathrm{soil}} = 1$), while
unpaved tracks soften like the ground around them. The memberships combine by fuzzy
AND~\cite{zadeh1965}, which vetoes any cell with a single impassable factor, as the improved map
of~\cite{liu2025} does:
\begin{equation}
  \mu_c = \min\left(\mu^{\mathrm{slope}}_c,\ \mu^{\mathrm{cover}}_c,\ \mu^{\mathrm{soil}}_c\right).
  \label{eq:fuzzy}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Route Planning}
The map is converted to a graph in which each cell is linked to its eight neighbours, and NO-GO
cells ($\mu_c = 0$) are removed. The cost of the edge between cells $u$ and $v$ is the time to
cross it at a speed proportional to the membership:
\begin{equation}
  w(u,v) = d_{uv} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1/\mu_u + 1/\mu_v\right),
  \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
where $d_{uv}$ is 90\,m, or $90\sqrt{2}$\,m on a diagonal. Equation (\ref{eq:cost}) is ours:
each half of a step is charged at the speed of the cell it lies in. A trip planned on day $t$
for day $t+h$ proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[1)] forecast $\widehat{\mathrm{RCI}}_{t+h}$ with (\ref{eq:forecast}) and subtract $m_{b,h}$;
  \item[2)] compute $\mu_c$ for every cell with (\ref{eq:soil}) and (\ref{eq:fuzzy});
  \item[3)] build the graph with (\ref{eq:cost}) and find the least-cost route from the start
  with Dijkstra's algorithm~\cite{dijkstra1959};
  \item[4)] if no route reaches the destination, hold the trip back rather than send it over
  NO-GO ground.
\end{enumerate}
Dijkstra's algorithm finds routes of the same cost as the A* search of~\cite{liu2025} with an
admissible heuristic~\cite{hart1968}, and one search from a start serves every destination. For
travel-time routing, $1/\mu$ in (\ref{eq:cost}) is replaced by the time per km at the speed
of~(\ref{eq:speed}), scaled by the land-cover membership off road.

\subsection{Convoy Coordination}
In a convoy, each pass weakens the track; inverting (\ref{eq:passes}), the $n$-th vehicle
needs:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{VCI}(n) = \mathrm{VCI}_1 + (\mathrm{VCI}_{50} - \mathrm{VCI}_1)\ln n / \ln 50 ,
  \label{eq:wear}
\end{equation}
which is ours and follows directly from~(\ref{eq:passes}). Two modes are compared. In
follow-the-leader, every vehicle takes the leader's route. In the wear-aware mode, the vehicles
plan in turn: after each route, the pass count of its cells rises by one, and the next vehicle
plans on the updated map with $\mathrm{VCI}(n)$ in place of $\mathrm{VCI}_1$. The 3$\times$3-cell
staging areas at both ends, where vehicles assemble, are not worn.

\subsection{Dataset Description}
In this study, the following three datasets are utilized:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Pachmarhi Weather Record:} daily data for the Pachmarhi plateau (Satpura range,
  1,067\,m) from 2015 to 2025 (4,018 days), with two-layer soil moisture, temperature and
  humidity from the ERA5-Land reanalysis~\cite{munoz2021}, wind, pressure and cloud cover from
  ERA5~\cite{hersbach2020}, and IMD gridded rainfall~\cite{pai2014}. As the IMD archive lags by
about a year, 2025 rainfall is from ERA5, whose day runs 00:00--24:00 rather than IMD's
08:30--08:30 IST. RCI is derived with~(\ref{eq:rci}); it falls through
  the vehicle thresholds every monsoon (Fig.~\ref{fig:rci}).
  \item \emph{Vehicle Attribute Dataset:} a public list of 2,451 records compiled from a
  simulation game's files, of which mass, engine power and top speed are used. Non-vehicle
  entries are removed by name, the running gear is read from the name, and 111 masses left at
  the file's default are replaced with approximate public figures. 1,145 ground vehicles remain
  (1,004 tracked, 125 wheeled, 16 half-tracks). As masses are per-template values, results are
  reported for five $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ bands (A: $<$18, B: 18--21, C: 21--24, D: 24--30, E: $\geq$30
  psi).
  \item \emph{Terrain Data:} a Google Earth Engine export with a 30\,m elevation model,
  ESA WorldCover~\cite{zanaga2022} and seasonal Sentinel-2 NDVI, aggregated to a 90\,m grid of
  76,218 cells, and 403 OpenStreetMap road segments~\cite{osm}. The export also holds
  Sentinel-1 radar and land surface temperature, which are not used: under this much canopy the
  radar signal mostly measures vegetation rather than soil wetness.
\end{enumerate}
The record is split chronologically into Training (2015--21), Validation (2022--23) and
Testing (2024--25), with 2,498, 730 and 724 forecast origins.

\subsection{Evaluation Setup}
Forecasts are made from every test day, and every input at a forecast origin is observed on or
before that day. The safety margins are tuned on one test year and scored on the other, in
both directions, so no reported margin is tuned on the days it is scored on.

For route planning, 6 start points are spread over one end of the largest connected road network
and 5 destinations over the far end, giving 30 trips of at least 8\,km. For every test day, band
and lead time $h \in \{1, 3, 7\}$, each planner plans the 30 trips on its own estimate of the
ground, and each route is scored on the true ground of the travel day against the best route
planned on that ground. Four planners are compared: terrain only (soil ignored), today's map
(planning on the current day's conditions, as the dynamic map of~\cite{liu2025} does, with our
memberships), today's map with a cross-year tuned margin, and the forecast with its margin. Variants of the proposed planner replace the fuzzy AND
by a product, by the equal-weight mean with a veto of~\cite{liu2025}, and by the same mean without
one. A trade-off sweep scales both margins by 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 and 3. Convoys of ten vehicles
of bands D and E, the heavy tracked and wheeled bands, are run on soft test days, when the
leader can pass but wear matters, with RCI between $1.05\,\mathrm{VCI}_1$ and
$1.5\,\mathrm{VCI}_{50}$ (up to 30 days per band, 3 trips each). Uncertainty is given as 95\%
confidence intervals (CI) from a moving-block bootstrap over test days, with 14-day blocks for
the day-to-day correlation and 2,000 resamples.

\subsection{Performance Metrics}
Each trafficability call is a binary classification of a vehicle-day, with NO-GO as the
positive class, giving True Positives (TP), False Negatives (FN), False Positives (FP) and
True Negatives (TN). The following metrics are employed:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[a)] \emph{Recall:} $\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN})$
  \item[b)] \emph{Precision:} $\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP})$
  \item[c)] \emph{False NO-GO calls:} FP per vehicle-year
  \item[d)] \emph{RMSE} and $R^2 = 1 - \sum(y - \hat y)^2 / \sum(y - \bar y)^2$ of the forecast
\end{enumerate}
As the margin~(\ref{eq:decision}) is tuned to 90\% recall, decisions are compared on false
NO-GO calls at equal recall. A planned trip is \emph{stuck} if its route crosses a NO-GO cell
on the travel day, and \emph{held} if no route is planned although one existed; completed trips
are also scored on their extra travel time over the best route.

\subsubsection{Model Selection}
Twenty-one forecasting models and rules were tested on the same inputs and years
(Table~\ref{tab:arch}), with the deep models averaged over three seeds. The GRU--Ridge
forecaster has the second-lowest RMSE averaged over the seven lead times (14.64\,psi) and the
fewest false NO-GO calls of the deep models at 7 days, with fewer parameters than the TFT and
N-BEATS. The GRU and the ridge regression alone are each less accurate than their average at
every lead time, a bidirectional GRU with attention is less accurate than the plain GRU, and
using a quantile model's 10th percentile (P10) instead of its median makes more false NO-GO
calls at equal recall. The ensemble averages the three models best on the validation years
(TCN, Transformer and GRU). The GRU--Ridge forecaster is
therefore used in the rest of the evaluation.

\subsubsection{Route Planning}
\label{sec:res-route}
Over 325,800 trips per planner, planning on terrain alone sends 35.5\% of trips into mud.
At 7 days, planning on the forecast beats planning on today's map on both errors: 2.9\% against
4.0\% of trips stuck (27\% fewer, 95\% CI 11--54\%) and 4.5\% against 5.8\% held back (CI 6--37\%
fewer). At 3 days it sends 1.6\% against 2.1\% into mud (24\% fewer) and holds back 2.3\% against
3.0\%, but both intervals include zero. A margin on today's map is slightly safer at its tuned
setting but holds back nearly twice as many trips: the forecast holds back 25--62\% fewer at 3
days and 27--61\% fewer at 7 days (95\% CI).
Sweeping both margins (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}), at an equal stuck rate the forecast holds back
40\% fewer trips at 3 days (CI 3--67\%) and 31\% fewer at 7 days, where the interval
($-$2--69\%) includes zero. As the forecaster sees no weather forecast, no planner foresees the
day the monsoon breaks; the gain comes in the days around the transitions. Completed routes are on average within
0.1\% of the best travel time. Among the variants, the product rule performs the same as the
fuzzy AND (1.6\% stuck at 3 days), the weighted mean with a veto of~\cite{liu2025} sends 1.8\%
into mud but takes 10.4\% longer, and the weighted mean without a veto sends 34.8\%. Routing on
the travel time of~(\ref{eq:speed}) instead of on $\mu_c$ shortens trips by about 5\% at the
same stuck rate (30.8 against 32.4 minutes per trip at 3 days), and the travel time predicted at
planning is on average within 0.3\% (3 days) and 1.0\% (7 days) of the time on the travel day,
with 9 trips in 10 within $-$2.1\% to $+$3.4\%.

\subsubsection{Convoy Coordination}
For heavy tracked vehicles (band D), a convoy of ten that follows the leader's route gets 5.5
vehicles through on average, as the lead track fails under the vehicles behind; the wear-aware
mode gets 9.2 through. Wheeled vehicles (band E) gain less (6.8 to 7.3), as the forest leaves
them few parallel lanes.

\subsection{Limitations}
Soil strength is computed from modelled ERA5-Land soil moisture for one 9\,km cell, not measured
with a cone penetrometer, and its spread by wetness index has no field check. Vehicle masses come
from a simulation game's files and the running gear is assumed, so results are reported by band.
The pass and wear curves between the published 1-pass and 50-pass points are ours, and speeds are
upper bounds. Margins tuned on the two test years differ by up to 7.25\,psi; the cross-year
scores include this cost, but a deployed system would need them re-checked as years are added.
With two test years, several 3-day and 7-day differences have wide intervals. Field cone-index
readings at the site are the first next step.
