\begin{abstract}
This work presents an integrated framework for forecasting terrain trafficability and planning safe routes for military ground vehicles. A hybrid GRU–Ridge model predicts soil strength using historical weather and soil-moisture data over a 1–7-day horizon. The predictions are combined with vehicle-specific mobility requirements and terrain characteristics to classify terrain and plan routes that avoid impassable ground, accounting for convoy-induced soil degradation. Experiments on the Pachmarhi plateau achieve an \(R^2\) of 0.922 for soil strength and, compared with current-condition planning, reduce trips encountering impassable terrain by 27\% and trips held back unnecessarily by 22\%, all at seven days. The framework supports predictive, risk-aware military mobility planning under changing environmental conditions.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Terrain trafficability, Soil strength forecasting, GRU–Ridge model, Vehicle Cone Index
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Military ground mobility depends on vehicle characteristics and changing terrain conditions. Rainfall and soil-moisture variations can reduce soil strength, rendering planned routes impassable. However, existing trafficability methods largely assess current conditions rather than predict future terrain accessibility~\cite{khan2021,liu2025}.

The Rating Cone Index (RCI) measures soil bearing strength, while the Vehicle Cone Index (VCI) represents vehicle-specific soil strength requirements. Although these indices underpin established mobility models~\cite{rula1971,haley1979}, integrating soil-strength forecasting with vehicle-specific trafficability assessment and route optimization remains a challenge. Machine-learning methods offer promising tools for forecasting soil conditions~\cite{wang2024hess,wang2024agwat}.

This study proposes an integrated framework combining a hybrid Gated Recurrent Unit–Ridge model for 1–7-day RCI forecasting with vehicle-specific VCI, terrain characteristics, and Dijkstra-based route planning. The main contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A forecast of soil strength for the day of travel, 1–7 days ahead, instead of planning on current conditions and assuming that they persist. The hybrid GRU–Ridge forecaster is lightweight, with a sixth of the parameters of the only tested model with a lower average error.
  \item The grouping of vehicles into five bands (A--E) by their one-pass VCI, where each band is represented by its median requirements and receives its own safety margin against forecast error per lead time, so that decisions are made per vehicle class at a fixed miss rate even when individual vehicle data are incomplete.
  \item A classification of terrain into GO, SLOW-GO, and NO-GO for each band, with SLOW-GO quantified by the number of vehicles one track can carry and the speed the soil allows.
  \item Fuzzy trafficability maps for each band on the day of travel, which combine the forecast soil strength with slope, land cover, and roads, Dijkstra-based routing on these maps, and a wear-aware convoy planner in which each vehicle plans its route on the track left by those before it.
  \item A daily soil-strength record for 2015--2025 derived from soil moisture and rainfall, and the cone indices of 1,145 vehicles computed with the NRMM Mobility Index and checked against published values.
  \item An evaluation using meteorological, vehicle, and geospatial data from the Pachmarhi plateau, which compares the forecaster with 21 other forecasting models and rules, and in which planning on the forecasts sends fewer trips into impassable terrain and holds back fewer unnecessarily than planning on current conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Works}

Military terrain trafficability assessment commonly relies on the RCI and VCI, supported by established models such as the NATO Reference Mobility Model \cite{haley1979,rula1971,ciobotaru2009}. Recent studies have developed terrain trafficability maps using environmental factors and route-planning algorithms \cite{liu2025}. GIS-based multi-criteria decision analysis maps off-road trafficability or land capability from elevation, land cover and soil data \cite{khan2021,dutta2024,mema2026}, path-planning studies select routes over road networks under uncertain conditions \cite{gao2019,xu2025}, and lightweight detectors classify military vehicles on edge devices \cite{gupta2022} (Table~\ref{tab:related-work}). Meanwhile, machine-learning methods, including recurrent neural networks and Transformers, have been applied to soil-moisture forecasting \cite{wang2024hess,wang2024agwat}.

Despite these advances, forecasting soil strength and directly integrating it with vehicle-specific mobility requirements and future route planning remains challenging. Existing approaches often assess current terrain conditions without adequately accounting for changes before deployment. The present study addresses this gap by linking the soil-strength forecast directly to vehicle-specific requirements and route planning, including terrain degradation during convoy movement.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}
The problem addressed in this study is to predict future terrain trafficability and determine safe, efficient routes for heterogeneous ground vehicles under changing environmental conditions. This requires estimating soil bearing strength several days in advance, relating it to each vehicle's mobility requirements, and accounting for terrain slope, land cover, road accessibility, and degradation from repeated vehicle use.

A central challenge is balancing operational safety with mobility: overly optimistic assessments risk sending vehicles into impassable terrain, while overly conservative assessments unnecessarily restrict movement. The objective is therefore to develop a unified framework that forecasts soil strength over a 1--7-day horizon, identifies vehicle-specific GO, SLOW-GO, and NO-GO conditions, and plans routes that minimize travel cost while reducing immobilization risk and unnecessary mission delays.

Each vehicle band $b$ is classified for day $t+h$ from the forecast soil strength $\widehat{\mathrm{RCI}}_{t+h}$, less a safety margin $m_{b,h}$:
\begin{equation}
  c_{b,t+h} =
  \begin{cases}
    \text{NO-GO}, & \widehat{\mathrm{RCI}}_{t+h} - m_{b,h} < \mathrm{VCI}_1, \\
    \text{SLOW-GO}, & \widehat{\mathrm{RCI}}_{t+h} - m_{b,h} < \mathrm{VCI}_{50}, \\
    \text{GO}, & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:decision}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ and $\mathrm{VCI}_{50}$ are the soil strengths the band needs for one and
for 50 passes in one track (Section~\ref{sec:approach}).

\subsection{Terrain--Vehicle Interaction Model}

\subsubsection{Soil Strength}
With the 0--7\,cm and 7--28\,cm soil moisture scaled to $s^{(1)}_t, s^{(2)}_t \in [0,1]$ between
20 and 49\,vol\% (the 11-year range, rounded) and $R^{3d}_t$, the rainfall of the last three days:
\begin{gather}
  \mathrm{SWI}_t = 0.35\,s^{(1)}_t + 0.65\,s^{(2)}_t , \\
  \mathrm{RCI}_t = 300\,e^{-k\,\mathrm{SWI}_t}
  \left(1 - 0.15\min\!\left(R^{3d}_t / 50\,\mathrm{mm},\ 1\right)\right),
  \label{eq:rci}
\end{gather}
where $\mathrm{SWI}_t$ is the soil wetness index and $k = \ln(300/15)$. As in the strength--moisture model used in~\cite{liu2025},
$\log(\mathrm{RCI})$ falls linearly with moisture. The constants are ours: the air-dry 300 lies in
the 100--500\, psi excess strength beyond which NRMM treats fine-grained soil as a hard
surface~\cite{haley1979}, and the saturated 15 follows field readings at
immobilization~\cite{rosca2018}. The deeper layer weighs more as it covers both NRMM critical
layers, 0--15\,cm for one pass and 15--30\,cm for 50~\cite{haley1979}; weights of 0.25/0.75 or
0.5/0.5 change the daily class on at most 2\% of vehicle-days. The rain factor adds free surface
water, which daily soil moisture misses; its 50\,mm is the top of the 25--50\,mm rainfall class
of~\cite{liu2025}, and its 15\% cap is below their 20--50\% reductions because soil moisture
already carries most of the rain's effect.

\subsubsection{Vehicle Cone Index}
The Mobility Index (MI)~\cite{rula1971,haley1979} of a tracked vehicle is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MI} = \left(\frac{\mathrm{CPF}\cdot\mathrm{WF}}{\mathrm{TF}\cdot\mathrm{GF}}
  + \mathrm{BF} - \mathrm{CF}\right)\mathrm{EF}\cdot\mathrm{TrF},
\end{equation}
from the contact pressure, weight, track, grouser, bogie, clearance, engine and transmission
factors, and gives the strength needed for one and for 50 passes in one track~\cite{ciobotaru2009}:
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{VCI}_1    &= 7.0 + 0.2\,\mathrm{MI} - 39.2/(\mathrm{MI} + 5.6), \\
  \mathrm{VCI}_{50} &= 19.27 + 0.43\,\mathrm{MI} - 125.79/(\mathrm{MI} + 7.08).
\end{align}
Wheeled vehicles use the wheeled forms~\cite{rosca2017,rosca2018} with 15\% tire deflection and
automatic transmissions; half-tracks take the worse of the track unit (tracked) and the front
axle (wheeled, 25\% of the weight). As the data give only mass, power and top speed, track
dimensions are interpolated by mass through six published reference vehicles (M22 to Tiger~II)
and tires follow standard sizes per axle load (HMMWV to heavy 8$\times$8). The code reproduces
the published M1 example (MI 98.6, $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ 26.3, $\mathrm{VCI}_{50}$
60.5)~\cite{ciobotaru2009} and 24 published 8$\times$8 values to within
1.7\,psi~\cite{rosca2017}. Vehicles fall into five $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ bands set by the fleet mix
(A: light tracked, B--D: medium to heavy tracked, E: wheeled), each represented by its median
$\mathrm{VCI}_1$, $\mathrm{VCI}_{50}$, power-to-weight ratio, contact pressure and top speed.

\subsubsection{Passes and Speed}
The classes of (\ref{eq:decision}) carry the labels of military terrain analysis~\cite{khan2021}, NO-GO below
$\mathrm{VCI}_1$ follows from its definition~\cite{rula1971}, and GO at $\mathrm{VCI}_{50}$ is our
mapping. SLOW-GO is quantified by the vehicles one track carries:
\begin{equation}
  n(\mathrm{RCI}) = 50^{(\mathrm{RCI} - \mathrm{VCI}_1)/(\mathrm{VCI}_{50} - \mathrm{VCI}_1)},
  \label{eq:passes}
\end{equation}
which is ours: only the 1-pass and 50-pass points are published~\cite{rula1971}, so the
requirement rises with $\ln n$ between them. The speed follows the NRMM fine-grained soil
relations~\cite{haley1979}, in which the resistance $R/W$, traction $T/W$ and slip $s$ depend on
$\mathrm{RCI} - \mathrm{VCI}_1$. On a slope $\theta$ the vehicle is stuck if its
traction cannot overcome the grade and the motion resistance,
$(T/W)\cos\theta < \sin\theta + (R/W)\cos\theta$; otherwise it moves at the speed at which its
power, less slip, overcomes them:
\begin{equation}
  v = \min\!\left(\frac{\eta P\,(1 - s)}{W\left(\sin\theta + (R/W)\cos\theta\right)},\ v_{\max}\right),
  \label{eq:speed}
\end{equation}
with $P$ the engine power, $W$ the weight and $v_{\max}$ the table's top speed; paved roads use
NRMM's hard-surface resistance. The constant power is ours, as the table has no gearbox data, and
$\eta = 0.8$ is an assumed driveline efficiency, to which speed is proportional and NO-GO calls
and passes are blind. The NO-GO line stays at $\mathrm{VCI}_1$, 0.7--2.1\,psi above NRMM's own
level-ground limit. At $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ the relations give the 20\% slip at which NRMM defines it,
and for the M1 they give 12\,km/h at $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ and 45\,km/h 20\,psi above, comparable to the
published NRMM run of about 20--56\,km/h~\cite{ciobotaru2009}. Speeds are upper bounds, as NRMM's
vegetation, ride and obstacle limits are not modeled.

\subsection{GRU--Ridge Forecaster}
Without numerical weather prediction, the forecaster reads 27 daily channels of the local log
(soil moisture, RCI, rainfall, Hargreaves evapotranspiration, meteorology,
antecedent precipitation indices with decay 0.85 and 0.95 and seasonal terms).
As RCI has a lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.99, it predicts the change for
all lead times $h = 1, \dots, 7$ days at once:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathrm{RCI}}_{t+h} = \mathrm{RCI}_t
  + \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\bar g_h(\mathbf{X}_{t-59:t}) + r_h(\boldsymbol{\phi}_t)\right],
  \label{eq:forecast}
\end{equation}
where $\bar g_h$ averages three GRU networks over a 60-day window, by which the slowest
antecedent index has decayed to 5\%, and $r_h$ is a ridge regression on 128 causal features
(settings in Table~\ref{tab:hyper}; all recurrent models in Table~\ref{tab:arch} share the GRU
size). Equation (\ref{eq:forecast}) is
ours: predicting the change makes persistence the zero-change forecast, and averaging a
nonlinear sequence model with a linear feature model lets their errors partly cancel. Comparing
the forecast directly with $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ calls a NO-GO day passable whenever the forecast errs
high: on the test years, a tenth of them at 1 day and a quarter at 7 days. The margin $m_{b,h}$ of
(\ref{eq:decision}) is a buffer against this error. The error is the same for every vehicle, but
it grows with $h$, and how often it changes a call depends on where the band's threshold lies:
through each monsoon the soil hovers around the $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ of
light tracked vehicles but stays well below that of wheeled ones (Fig.~\ref{fig:rci}). The margin
is therefore set per band and lead time: searched in 0.25\,psi steps up to 40\,psi, it is the
smallest that catches 90\% of NO-GO days. This rule is ours and fixes the miss rate per vehicle
class.

\subsection{Trafficability Mapping}
A 90\,m grid of 76,218 cells (3$\times$3 blocks of a 30\,m Google Earth Engine export) holds the
mean elevation and slope, the dominant land cover and the peak NDVI over three seasons; cells that
are mostly water are impassable. The SWI of each cell is shifted by $\delta_c = 0.1\,
\mathrm{clip}(z_c, -2, 2)$, where $z_c$ is the z-score of its topographic wetness index, so that by (\ref{eq:rci})
$\mathrm{RCI}_c = \widehat{\mathrm{RCI}}\,e^{-k\delta_c}$. The scale is ours: 2.9\,vol\% per
standard deviation, capped at 5.8\,vol\%. The soil membership is:
\begin{equation}
  \mu^{\mathrm{soil}}_c =
  \begin{cases}
    0, & \mathrm{RCI}_c < \mathrm{VCI}_1, \\
    0.25 + 0.75\min(\rho_c, 1), & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:soil}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_c = (\mathrm{RCI}_c - \mathrm{VCI}_1)/(\mathrm{VCI}_{50} - \mathrm{VCI}_1)$. Equation
(\ref{eq:soil}) is ours: the NRMM speed at $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ is 17--25\% of that at
$\mathrm{VCI}_{50}$ for the band vehicles, hence the floor of 0.25, and the linear rise is within
0.1 of the NRMM curve at the midpoint. Slope and land-cover memberships follow the tables
of~\cite{liu2025}, with the slope steps smoothed into a monotone curve. As 79\% of the plateau is
tree cover, which~\cite{liu2025} scores as impassable, forest is mapped by peak NDVI to their
forest ($\geq$0.75, about the upper tercile; 0), bush-wood (0.60--0.75; 0 wheeled, 0.2 tracked) and
grassland rows ($<$0.60; 0.4 wheeled, 0.6 tracked). Water scores 0, and OpenStreetMap
roads from motorway to track score 1 for slope and cover, and paved ones also for soil. Before
soil, 26\% of the plateau is passable for wheeled and 72\% for tracked vehicles. The memberships
combine by fuzzy AND, which vetoes any single impassable factor, as the improved
map of~\cite{liu2025} does:
\begin{equation}
  \mu_c = \min\left(\mu^{\mathrm{slope}}_c,\ \mu^{\mathrm{cover}}_c,\ \mu^{\mathrm{soil}}_c\right).
  \label{eq:fuzzy}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Route Planning and Convoys}
Each cell is linked to its eight neighbors, NO-GO cells are removed, and an edge costs the time
to cross it at a speed proportional to $\mu$:
\begin{equation}
  w(u,v) = d_{uv} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1/\mu_u + 1/\mu_v\right),
  \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
where $d_{uv}$ is 90\,m, or $90\sqrt{2}$\,m on a diagonal; (\ref{eq:cost}) is ours and charges each
half step at the speed of its cell. A trip planned on day $t$ for day $t+h$ forecasts
$\widehat{\mathrm{RCI}}_{t+h}$ with (\ref{eq:forecast}), subtracts $m_{b,h}$, maps $\mu_c$ with
(\ref{eq:soil}) and (\ref{eq:fuzzy}), and finds the least-cost route with Dijkstra's
algorithm; if none reaches the destination, the trip is held back. For travel-time
routing, $1/\mu$ in (\ref{eq:cost}) becomes the time per km at the speed~(\ref{eq:speed}), scaled
off road by the cover membership. In a convoy each pass weakens the track; inverting
(\ref{eq:passes}), the $n$-th vehicle needs:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{VCI}(n) = \mathrm{VCI}_1 + (\mathrm{VCI}_{50} - \mathrm{VCI}_1)\ln n / \ln 50 .
  \label{eq:wear}
\end{equation}
In the wear-aware mode the vehicles plan in turn on the updated pass counts with
$\mathrm{VCI}(n)$ in place of $\mathrm{VCI}_1$; in follow-the-leader all take the leader's route.
The 3$\times$3-cell staging areas at both ends, where vehicles assemble, are not worn.

\section{Experimental Discussions}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Dataset Description}
In this study, the following three datasets are utilized:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Pachmarhi Weather Record:} daily data for the Pachmarhi plateau (Satpura range,
  1,067\,m), 2015--2025 (4,018 days): soil moisture, temperature and humidity from
  ERA5-Land~\cite{munoz2021}, wind, pressure and cloud cover from ERA5~\cite{hersbach2020}, and
  IMD gridded rainfall~\cite{pai2014}, replaced in 2025 by ERA5, whose day runs 00--24\,h rather
  than 08:30--08:30 IST.
  \item \emph{Vehicle Attribute Dataset:} 2,451 records compiled from a simulation game's files~\cite{warthunder},
  of which mass, power and top speed are used. After removing non-vehicles and replacing 111
  default masses with public figures, 1,145 ground vehicles remain (1,004 tracked, 125 wheeled,
  16 half-tracks); as masses are per-template values, results are reported by band (A: $<$18,
  B: 18--21, C: 21--24, D: 24--30, E: $\geq$30 psi).
  \item \emph{Terrain Data:} a Google Earth Engine~\cite{gorelick2017} export with a 30\,m elevation model, ESA
  WorldCover and seasonal Sentinel-2 NDVI, and 403 OpenStreetMap road
  segments. Its Sentinel-1 radar layers are not used: under this canopy they mostly
  measure vegetation.
\end{enumerate}
The record is split into Training (2015--21), Validation (2022--23) and Testing (2024--25), with
2,498, 730 and 724 forecast origins.

\subsection{Evaluation Setup and Metrics}
Forecasts use only inputs observed up to each test day, and margins are tuned on one test year
and scored on the other. With NO-GO as the positive class of each vehicle-day, recall
is fixed at 90\% by the margin, so decisions are compared on false NO-GO calls (FP per
vehicle-year); forecasts are scored by RMSE and $R^2$. Table~\ref{tab:hyper} gives the forecaster's settings
and its test error as the training loss: the mean squared error of the RCI change in units of its
training variance, on which persistence scores 0.82, 0.77 and 0.82 at 1, 3 and 7 days. For routing, 6 starts and 5 destinations at opposite ends of the largest road
network give 30 trips of at least 8\,km, planned for every test day, band and lead time
$h \in \{1,3,7\}$ and scored on the true ground of the travel day: \emph{stuck} if the route
crosses a NO-GO cell, \emph{held} if no route is planned although one existed, and the extra
time over the best route. The planners are terrain only, today's map (planning on current conditions
as~\cite{liu2025} does, with our memberships), today's map with a margin, and the forecast with
its margin; variants replace the fuzzy AND by a product or by the equal-weight mean
of~\cite{liu2025} with and without its veto, and a sweep scales both margins from 0 to 3 times. Convoys of ten band D and band E vehicles run on soft days with RCI between
$1.05\,\mathrm{VCI}_1$ and $1.5\,\mathrm{VCI}_{50}$ (up to 30 per band, 3 trips each). 95\%
confidence intervals (CI) come from a moving-block bootstrap over test days (14-day blocks,
2,000 resamples).

\subsubsection{Results by Vehicle Band}
Fig.~\ref{fig:rci} shows why decisions are made per band: the same soil strength crosses each
band's $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ at a different time. Each monsoon it drops by over 100\,psi within two
weeks to near the saturated strength and recovers over about two months, so that bands with a
higher $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ return to GO later; wheeled vehicles (band E) are NO-GO on 34\% of days
and light tracked ones (band A) on 13\% (Table~\ref{tab:slowgo}). On a typical SLOW-GO day only 2--5 vehicles can use one track, at 14--32\,km/h against
57--60\,km/h on dry ground (Table~\ref{tab:slowgo}); at 5\,psi above $\mathrm{VCI}_1$, a track
carries 1.5--2.3 vehicles at 12--16\,km/h. At equal recall, the forecast makes 23--45\% fewer
false NO-GO calls than the margin rule on persistence in every band, and pooled over all vehicles
21\% fewer at 3 days (CI 4--37\%) and 31\% at 7 days (CI 14--46\%). Light tracked vehicles
(band A) are the hardest case, as their $\mathrm{VCI}_1$ is close to the saturated strength: through each monsoon the soil hovers around their line

\subsubsection{Route Planning}
Over 325,800 trips per planner, terrain alone sends 35.5\% of trips into mud. At 7 days, the
forecast beats today's map on both errors: 2.9\% against 4.0\% stuck (27\% fewer, CI 11--54\%)
and 4.5\% against 5.8\% held (CI 6--37\% fewer). At 3 days it gives 1.6\% against 2.1\% stuck and
2.3\% against 3.0\% held, but both intervals include zero. A margin on today's map is slightly
safer at its tuned setting but holds back far more trips; the forecast holds back 47\% fewer at
both lead times (CI 25--62\% at 3 days, 27--61\% at 7 days). Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff} traces this trade-off as each margin is scaled: at both lead times the
forecast's curve lies below and to the left of that of today's map, and at an equal stuck rate it holds
back 40\% fewer trips at 3 days (CI 3--67\%) and 31\% at 7 days, where the interval ($-$2--69\%)
includes zero. Completed routes are within 0.1\% of the best travel time. The product rule
matches the fuzzy AND, the equal-weight mean with veto sends 1.8\% into mud at 3 days but takes
10.4\% longer, and without the veto 34.8\%. Routing on travel time shortens trips by about 5\% at
the same stuck rate (30.8 against 32.4 minutes at 3 days), with predicted times on average within
0.3\% (3 days) and 1.0\% (7 days) of the travel day's, and 9 in 10 within $-$2.1\% to $+$3.4\%.
With no weather forecast as input, no planner foresees the day the monsoon breaks; the gain comes
around the transitions.

A convoy of ten heavy tracked vehicles (band D) following its leader gets 5.5 through on average,

vehicles (band E) gain less (6.8 to 7.3), as the forest leaves them few parallel lanes.

\subsection{Limitations}
Soil strength is computed from modeled soil moisture for one 9\, km cell, not measured, and its
spread by wetness index is unchecked in the field. Vehicle masses come from game files and the
running gear is assumed, so results are reported by band. The pass and wear curves and the speed
assumptions are ours, so speeds are upper bounds. Margins tuned on the two test years differ by
up to 7.25\, psi, and two test years leave some intervals wide. Field cone-index readings at the
site are the next step.

\section{Conclusion}
This study presents an integrated framework for forecasting terrain trafficability and planning safe, efficient routes for heterogeneous military ground vehicles under changing environmental conditions. The proposed framework combines a hybrid Gated Recurrent Unit–Ridge (GRU–Ridge) model for 1–7-day soil strength forecasting with vehicle-specific cone index requirements, terrain characteristics, fuzzy trafficability mapping, and Dijkstra-based route optimization. Convoy coordination is further incorporated to account for the progressive degradation of soil bearing capacity caused by repeated vehicle passage.

Experiments using meteorological, vehicle, and geospatial data for the Pachmarhi plateau demonstrate the potential of the proposed approach. The GRU–Ridge model, with a sixth of the parameters of the only tested model with a lower average error, achieves an average RMSE of 14.64 psi across the seven forecast horizons and an \(R^2\) of 0.922 for soil strength at a seven-day horizon. Compared with route planning based on current terrain conditions, the forecast-based approach reduces the proportion of trips encountering impassable terrain by 27\% and of trips held back unnecessarily by 22\% at seven days. Furthermore, incorporating track degradation into convoy planning increases the average number of heavy tracked vehicles that complete a route from 5.5 to 9.2 out of 10. These findings highlight the benefits of integrating predictive soil strength assessment with vehicle-specific mobility constraints and route optimization.

Despite these encouraging results, the framework relies on modeled rather than field-measured soil strength, and some vehicle characteristics and track-degradation relationships are estimated from assumptions. Future work will focus on validating the predictions using field cone-index measurements, improving vehicle parameter estimation, incorporating numerical weather forecasts, and evaluating the framework across additional terrains and seasonal conditions. Overall, the proposed approach provides a promising basis for anticipatory, risk-aware military mobility planning under dynamic environmental conditions.
