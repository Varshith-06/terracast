Plan for the ground you will drive on, not the ground you see today

Forecast-aware off-road route planning for military vehicles, computed on the vehicle with no weather service and no network link
[Author One]1, [Author Two]1, [Author Three]2
1[Department, Institution, City]    2[Institution, City]

Routes planned on a forecast made on the vehicle sent 24% fewer trips into mud than routes planned on today's map, three days out, and held back fewer that could have gone. At the same risk, they hold back 31–40% fewer trips than a safety margin on today's map.

01The ground changes faster than the map

Off the road, the soil decides who moves. On the Pachmarhi plateau a monsoon week takes ground firmness from over 200 psi to under 20, so a route that is GO when planned can be NO-GO when the column arrives.

Maps describe the ground today [1]. We plan for the day of travel.

Fig. 1 Rain and ground firmness (rating cone index, RCI), 2024–25. Below its red line, a vehicle band is stuck.

02Firmness from soil moisture, limits from the vehicle

SWI = 0.35 s0–7 cm + 0.65 s7–28 cm
RCI = 300 · e−3.0 SWI · (1 − 0.15 min(R3d / 50 mm, 1))
NO-GO if RCI < VCI₁  ·  SLOW-GO if < VCI₅₀  ·  GO otherwise s: ERA5-Land soil moisture, scaled 0 (driest) to 1 (wettest) in 11 years. The wet end, 15 psi, follows field readings where a 4×4 bogged down [2].

Each of 1,145 ground vehicles gets its VCI₁ and VCI₅₀ from the NRMM Mobility Index [2, 3]. Our tracked version reproduces the published M1 example.

Fig. 2 The fleet in five firmness bands: light tracked (A) stuck on 10% of days, wheeled (E) on 30%.

03A forecast that fits on the vehicle

A GRU and a ridge regression predict the change in RCI 1–7 days ahead from the local weather log alone: 20% lower error than "tomorrow equals today" at 7 days.

A safety margin per band, tuned on one test year and scored on the other, turns it into a NO-GO call.

Fig. 3 Same 90% of NO-GO days caught, 23–45% fewer false alarms at 7 days: fewer days a unit stays parked for nothing.

04What it cannot see

Without a weather forecast, nothing predicts the day the monsoon breaks. The gain comes in the days around it.

Fig. 4 Monsoon onset, 2024: firmness three days ahead.

05Same trip, two plans

Fig. 5 A tracked vehicle from band D (VCI₁ 26 psi) plans on 15 June 2025 for a trip 7 days later. On the planning day the ground reads 128 psi and the direct route (orange) looks open. On the travel day it reads 34 psi and that route crosses NO-GO soil (×). The route planned on the forecast (blue, margin 19.5 psi) avoids it. Circle: start; square: end; grey lines: OpenStreetMap roads.

06Two monsoons, 325,800 trips

Every day of 2024–25, 30 trips of 8–25 km per band, leaving 1, 3 or 7 days after planning, checked against the real ground on the day of travel. A planner fails two ways: it sends a vehicle into mud, or holds back a trip that was possible.

Fig. 6 Both errors as each margin goes from none to 3× the tuned value (ringed). The forecast is closer to the corner at every setting.

08Convoys: share the track, not the map

Each pass churns soft ground. Of ten heavy tracked vehicles, 5.5 get through when they follow the leader and 9.2 when each re-plans on a shared pass-count map, for 6.9 kB of track updates.

Limits. Ground firmness is computed from modelled soil moisture (ERA5-Land, one 9 km cell), not measured. Spreading it over the map by wetness index is a heuristic with no field check. Vehicle masses come from a game-file dataset, with wheeled ones corrected from open sources, so we report bands, not named vehicles. Tuned margins differ between the two test years by up to 7.2 psi.
